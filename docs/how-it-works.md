# How it works

1. A Wav2Vec2 model fine-tuned on espeak labels (`facebook/wav2vec2-lv-60-espeak-cv-ft`) recognizes the sounds actually said, straight from the audio. No language model gets a chance to "correct" the learner.
2. The sentence is turned into sounds with the CMU pronouncing dictionary (English) or espeak-ng (the other languages), and these sounds are force-aligned on the frame posteriors of that model (CTC Viterbi, in numpy). Every expected sound gets a time span and a set of goodness-of-pronunciation scores: how far its posterior is from the best token on its frames, how much the sentence likelihood drops when it is deleted or replaced by a close sound, its duration and context.
3. A small model trained on [speechocean762](https://github.com/jimbozhang/speechocean762) (5,000 utterances rated by five experts, phone by phone and word by word) turns these scores, together with the recognizer's own representation of the sound (its hidden states on those frames, reduced to 16 dimensions), into the probability that each sound, then each word, is mispronounced. A word is reported above 0.35, the point of best F1 against the raters. The same alignment says whether the recording is a reading of the sentence at all.
4. Gradient-boosted trees trained on the same corpus map 53 utterance-level features (aggregated goodness of pronunciation, speech rate, pauses, rhythm, pitch and energy statistics) to the raters' four scores. Wrong and half-read sentences, absent from the corpus, were added as synthetic examples so that they score low.
5. The audio is also transcribed with `facebook/wav2vec2-large-960h` (English) or a language-specific XLSR checkpoint, for the transcription and the word error rate.
6. Unless `acoustic=False`, the sentence is synthesized (gTTS by default, Piper or Kokoro offline), both recordings are encoded with Wav2Vec2 and aligned with DTW: that is the acoustic distance, which feeds `formula_score`.
7. Pitch (pYIN) and RMS energy give the prosody curves.

On the speechocean762 test split (2,500 utterances, speakers unseen in training), the scores reach Pearson 0.79 with the human total, 0.74 with accuracy, 0.82 with fluency and 0.81 with prosody; the formula of the previous versions reached 0.63. Of the words flagged, 53 % are rejected by the raters (20 % before), for 51 % of the words they reject (73 % before), with one word in twenty flagged instead of one in five. Details, scripts and the comparison of alternatives (WavLM, ZIPA, logistic variants) in [benchmarks/](benchmarks/README.md). Models are plain JSON files in `openpronounce/models/`, retrainable with `benchmarks/word_model.py` and `benchmarks/scorer.py`. The other languages run on generic models, trained on the same corpus without the identity of the sounds or the hidden states, on the list of recognizer tokens each language accepts for a sound of espeak (`Language.variants`) and on a reporting threshold of its own (`Language.word_threshold`), both chosen on native speakers. Their scorer reads the alignment through a native reference (`models/native_<lang>.json`): the gap between native readers of the language and native readers of English is taken out first, so that what the recognizer hears differently in Dutch is not counted as an accent. Free speech follows the same path in every language, on the transcript instead of the text. A word that was not said is found by a third small model, speech in excess by counting the sounds heard where none was expected; both are measured on sentences whose text was altered (`benchmarks/missing_extra.py`). The original idea is described in [this blog post](https://blog.lepine.pro/en/ai-wav2vec-pronunciation-vectorization/).
